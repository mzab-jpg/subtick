Expanding the Radius of Daily Life

BY PACKY McCORMICK · TSUNG XU // 18 MIN READ

The speed of transportation has historically governed the size of our cities, the depth of our human connections, and the geographic reach of opportunity.

Throughout history, the effective diameter of a human community has hovered stubbornly around what Marchetti termed the one-hour travel budget: thirty minutes out, thirty minutes back. When walking was our only velocity, cities spanned four miles. When horses and trains arrived, they stretched to twenty.

Automobiles decentralized the world, but in doing so, they also clogged it. Today, the fundamental physics of congestion imposes a tax on our ambition. We spend precious cognitive bandwidth navigating bumper-to-bumper inertia.

Point-to-point electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) does not simply make wealthy commuters faster; it rewrites the geometric topology of human civilization. When a 60-minute highway grind compresses into an eight-minute whisper through the lower sky, the boundary of daily life expands by an order of magnitude.

CORE THESIS

Physical distance is static; human access is a dynamic function of velocity and friction.

As battery densities cross the 400 Wh/kg threshold and autonomous flight corridors mature, the economics of daily flight pivot from private aviation luxury into mass transit utility.
